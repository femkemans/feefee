When a small shoot grows.

it starts as one photo day. then there's a video, a producer, a crew of thirty, and no way back. nobody prepares you for it, so here's what to do at each step.

a seven minute read

01

the signs

Notice it early.

  • more people keep joining the calls.
  • "can we also film it?" a video gets added to a photo job.
  • a producer appears, or the client asks you to find one.
  • one day becomes three, with prep, a recce and a travel day.

any one of these means the job you quoted is not the job you're doing anymore.

02

re-quote

A bigger job is a new quote.

don't let the old number stretch. send a new quote as soon as the job changes, before you say yes to the new parts. it feels awkward, and it's normal.

a line you can copyhappy to see this grow. since we're now adding [video / extra days / a bigger crew], here's an updated quote, so we're on the same page before we go further.

on your desk you can duplicate your first quote and add the new lines.

03

who does what

Your role can change too.

when a crew grows, roles split. a producer takes budget, crew and planning. an art director can become the creative director, with more responsibility and a different fee. ask out loud: "what is my role now, and what is not mine anymore?"

write it down in one mail, so nobody expects you to do three jobs for one fee.

04

multi-day shoots

Every day counts, not only the shoot days.

  • shoot days: your day rate, each day.
  • prep, recce and travel days: often a lower rate, but never free.
  • options and holds: if they hold dates, agree how long, and what happens if they drop them late.
  • overtime: agree how long a day is (often 10 hours) and what an extra hour costs.

words like hold, recce and overtime are explained in set words.

05

film is different

Film and commercial work run on different rules.

commercial jobs are short and well paid per day, with usage on top. film jobs run longer, sometimes at a lower day rate, with a deal memo instead of a quote, a weekly rate, a credit in the titles and less talk about usage.

if a photographer joins a feature film, ask for the deal memo early, check the hours per week, and ask how the credit will read.

06

insurance

Ask who insures what.

  • your own liability insurance: covers you if you damage something or someone gets hurt because of your work. many freelancers have it; big productions often ask for it.
  • your kit: camera, lights, the whole case. check if it's covered away from home and abroad.
  • production insurance: usually the producer or client arranges it for the shoot itself. ask, don't assume.
07

the messy end

A wrap list, for when everyone wants to go home.

  • final invoice sent, with every extra day and expense on it.
  • usage confirmed in writing: where, how long, which countries. see usage and buyouts, plainly.
  • files delivered, and what you keep for your portfolio agreed.
  • credits agreed, spelled right.
  • one line asking to be kept in the loop if it grows again.

this is a plain guide, not legal or insurance advice. rules differ per country. for a big contract, ask a lawyer or your trade union to read it first.

in the middle of a job that's growing? ask. someone has been there.
